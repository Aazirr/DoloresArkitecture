Hiring an architect is unlike hiring most professionals. The relationship typically lasts one to three years, involves dozens of decisions that will affect how you live or work for decades, and requires a level of trust that's hard to establish from a portfolio alone.

Here is what we've observed — from our own practice and from clients who've worked with other firms before coming to us — about what separates a productive client–architect relationship from a frustrating one.

## Look at completed buildings, not just renders

Architectural renders are persuasive. Finished buildings are honest. Ask to visit a firm's completed projects, or at minimum to speak with past clients. Pay attention to how the building is holding up: are the details well-executed? Is the material palette coherent in person, not just in photographs?

## Understand how they communicate

Architecture involves continuous decision-making. You'll be asked to choose between options you may not fully understand, on timelines that feel unreasonably short. A firm that communicates clearly — that explains trade-offs in plain terms without condescension — is worth more than one that produces beautiful drawings but leaves you confused about what you're approving.

Ask how they prefer to communicate (meetings, email, messaging apps), how often you should expect updates, and who your day-to-day contact will be.

## Discuss fees openly and early

Architectural fees in the Philippines typically range from 6 to 12 percent of construction cost for a full-service residential commission, depending on scope and complexity. Some firms charge flat fees; others use hourly rates for smaller projects.

There is no fee structure that is inherently better than another, but you should understand exactly what is included and what would trigger additional charges. Reimbursable expenses, permit fees, and consultant coordination are common areas of ambiguity.

## Trust matters more than portfolio

If a firm's portfolio doesn't excite you, keep looking. But if their work speaks to you and you don't trust them — don't trust that they'll listen, that they'll be honest about problems, that they'll advocate for your interests with contractors — then the portfolio won't save the project.

The best architecture comes from a relationship where both sides feel free to say what they actually think.
